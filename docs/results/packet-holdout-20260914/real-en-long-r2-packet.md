# Question: Produce a cautious evidence-backed briefing on the pilot's measured outcomes and its unresolved limits.

## Answer

The pilot shows that displayed wait bands matched actual waits for a majority of included visits, but it does not show that the display improved outcomes compared with having no display [S1][S2]. Accessibility testing found both successful screen-reader announcements and specific configuration or translation barriers, but the review was too small and narrow to support a population-wide conclusion [S2]. The evidence can inform further testing, but it does not justify a citywide effectiveness, equity, savings, or deployment claim [S1][S2]. A decision should require evidence of causal benefit, representative accessibility, operational reliability, and an approved implementation case before expansion (no source) (judgment).

## Evidence

The pilot ran in three municipal library branches from 2026-06-01 through 2026-08-15 and included 4,860 visits; 71% reached a desk within the displayed band, with branch results ranging from 66% to 75% [S1].

The study documented whether waits fell within the selected band, but it had no comparison period from before the display and therefore cannot establish the display’s effect [S1][S2].

The materials do not establish whether the display reduced queue abandonment because they provide neither a relevant baseline nor complete counts and trends for people who left the queue [S1][S2].

Staff recorded 58 display corrections, while the available source notes do not fully reproduce the claimed category-level breakdown and say that correction labels were retained as staff records rather than independently audited findings [S1][S2].

Correction records do not demonstrate that visitors received incorrect service or that the software failed, and the proposed clearer staffing-change notice has no quantified predicted effect [S1][S2].

In 18 volunteer accessibility sessions, configured screen readers announced the band in 15; two sessions involved an extension suppressing the live update, and one involved a changed display language without a translated label [S2].

Those observations demonstrate successes and specific barriers only in the tested setups, not a general accessibility rate or universal inaccessibility [S2].

Because the retained records contain no visitor demographics, the pilot cannot establish equal usefulness across visitor groups [S1][S2].

The three-branch results are not a citywide estimate, and the materials contain no approved contract, vendor selection, rollout date, or projected citywide benefit; broader replacement requires a separate budget decision [S1][S2].

The protocol did not randomize visitors, compare alternative displays, or estimate effects on staffing demand [S2].

## Counter-evidence and limits

S1 reports the matching result, while both sources limit causal and citywide interpretations [S1][S2]. The reported branch range alone does not establish why branches differed [S1]. The accessibility review used volunteers in simulated, observed sessions and did not represent all devices, languages, assistive technologies, or unassisted visitors [S2]. The sources lack visitor demographics, abandonment counts and trends, and representative accessibility coverage [S1][S2]. Both sources are hosted on the same fixture domain, limiting independent corroboration [S1][S2].

## Next actions

1. Run a pre-specified comparison that measures outcomes with and without the display, including abandonment and staffing demand (no source) (judgment).
2. Test translated labels and live updates across representative devices, languages, browser extensions, assistive technologies, and unassisted use (no source) (judgment).
3. Retain a staffed alternative while measuring its use, confusion reduction, and staffing cost (no source) (judgment).
4. Require an accessibility and operations review, budget approval, vendor decision, and benefit case before considering citywide procurement (no source) (judgment).
